The City

From ages 19 to 22 I was focused on acquiring money from Smith College in order to visit cities. I was lucky and successful in this endeavor and visited the following cities on Smith’s dime: Mexico City, Atlanta, Montreal, Vienna, Sydney, Seoul, Tokyo, Jakarta, and Istanbul. In each city, I felt my posture correct. In each city, I mapped out routes from design centers to leftist bookstores to trending bakeries to contemporary art museums on Google Maps and then set out for the day. Especially in my last year of college, when I was isolated and depressed, it felt better than any one could even imagine to walk in the Christmas Market in Vienna. It was revelatory to sit next to the canal in a scarf or to go with German girls to a club. The only way I could think of describing it at the time was that the city felt like my real life.

"I had come to this city to appear to myself, to seek some kind of new language." Lisa Roberston, The Baudelaire Fractal.

New York City is dense with preconceptions, deeply packed muddy layers. It’s difficult to move through the city without being aware that you are in New York City, to step each step with a squelch. A squelch that sounds like Friends and Girls and Sex and the City, in addition to Following Your Dreams in the Greatest City in the World. I walk to the pier on my lunch break and see the Statue of Liberty. How can I ever see it as just a statue, instead of a myth or a keychain? #I CANT GET OVER THIS VIEW 🤩

I push and push, then coast, then push and push through the city. On rollerblades, right foot left foot, tripped by a crack in the sidewalk. It often feels unclear what I’m supposed to be doing, on the larger scale, but on the day to day I seek to be engaged in the most optimal activity for each hour every hour. When I talk to people, strangers and friends and coworkers and kids and acquaintances and acquaintances’ dogs, I allow myself to forget about both the large and small scales

I litter money around the city; indulgence is easy and encouraged. Some recent purchases include: diner hash browns, fly-sticking paper, fly-trapping lights, an espresso tonic with real whipped cream, a handmade backpack from a bike store, shampoo and conditioner from a friendly man in a store with many bottles, admission to my friend’s boyfriend’s figure drawing class, expensive asparagus.

I seek out the $3, the $4, the $5, the $6, and it usually ends up being $12 no matter what I do. I trade vast swaths of my time for the pride and pleasure and privilege to pay $12. The time I sell is more often than not passive; I spend the day in the cafe copying and pasting information from one platform to another or writing code which feels like solving puzzles and then chatting or waiting around or reading interesting articles.

I also tutor math, paid and unpaid, to girls in many areas of Brooklyn and Queens: Park Slope, Flatbush, Crown Heights, Bushwick, Ridgewood, Clinton Hill. Some of them are wealthy and some are not. Some girls are given lessons in singing to prepare for Broadway camp and croissants every day after school. Some girls live in my neighborhood. I feel a real affection for all of the girls, for how they push through the city, for how they are sometimes lazy but often make me laugh. One begs me to test her on her 7 times tables even though right now she only knows 7 times 1 and 7 times 2. I want them all to have a pet rabbit and a trip to Paris, if they desire these things. As New York City gets warmer and wetter, and the system strains, I want them all to stay cool and sheltered.

Many people’s connection to survival is tenuous. I find it incredibly hard to believe that we all accept that rent must be paid, enormous sums of it each month. Entire neighborhoods disappear and the disappearance keeps rolling eastward. The critiques I can deal towards myself come easily and plentiful; I know my place in this whole situation. I tried to find the cheapest rent and now I try to help my neighbors.

> It’s easy to incorporate strange or jarring or even beautiful sights, sounds, and experience into the normal in the city. In fact, this is what you are trained to do. Anything else would prove overloading. The out of ordinary must be turned ordinary. You are trying to get to work on time. You must look at your phone, the game Cantaloupe, and focus on combining blueberries into dragonfruit, dragonfruits into kiwis, kiwis into melons, melons into grapefruits, grapefruits into cantaloupes, and so forth. I resist this sometimes, and then other times I don’t.

I like the regulars at the cafe and I notice when they shave. I realized recently that every place in the city has regulars. People are always greeting each other like, How are you doing, buddy. The travel vlogger comes in with his enormous dog. I turn to my coworker: I thought that dog moved to Ohio.

I met a dear friend at a party where I spoon-fed him mashed potatoes. On weekend nights, me and him flail in dark spaces, and strangers tell us Now that’s real dancing, wow you guys know how to Dance. I get soaked through, wet to the outside of my cutoff denim shorts, and I glow with the amazing happiness of finding my one true dance partner and the pride of being good at something. He sniffs poppers and I slide my shades down, shaking my hair vigorously to feel a delicious scalp sensation.

I like to look up in the city – I like to see where a building or a tree meets the blue blue or blue gray sky. In Red Hook, this meeting often feels meaningful in one way or another.

In London last summer with my dad and sister we sat down to eat in a dark restaurant. There was a man sitting next to us, enjoying a steak with mashed potatoes or something else typically British like that, and he asked us if we had traveled from Boston that morning. Yes, we replied with surprise. How did you know? Because, he said, pausing for effect with satisfaction, I was your pilot.

I would love to be a boy in the city, especially one with a cross body bag who crosses the city on bike. I would go so fast on my bike as a boy. I wouldn’t grab the handle bars but instead lace my fingers behind my head and lean back and maybe weave in between cars but never get injured except for some scrapes. When I go on a date with a girl I would imagine that she would feel a thrill at getting to date me, me with a cool haircut and an occupation like woodworker/butcher/designer/DJ. I would have large, deft hands. I would probably wear a striped button down, or a worn T shirt.

I look like others, I know that – I try not to pay attention to clothes as signs & signified anymore, but it’s hard not to. The clothes I wear spell out a message. I am legible. I see clothes rhyming all across the city. Freitag bags, slim fit pumas. I'll never win this game. I wear clothes like a dalmatian would. It makes me not even want to try sometimes, but recently I’ve been seized by the urge for a strong silhouette. I want to have a stronger shape.
